ADA website compliance checklist for online stores

The checks that matter most, in plain language

US courts and settlements measure a store's website against WCAG 2.2 Level AA. The full standard is long and technical, so this checklist puts the parts that matter most for an online store in plain language. Work through it page by page, starting with the path a shopper takes to buy: home page, a collection, a product page, the cart and checkout.

1.Images and media

  • Every product image has a short description (alt text) that tells a shopper what it shows, such as "Navy linen shirt, front view."
  • Purely decorative images are marked so screen readers skip them.
  • Text that appears inside an image, like a sale banner, is also available as real text.
  • Videos have captions, and videos that carry information without words have a description.
  • Nothing plays sound automatically, or there is an easy way to stop it.

2.Color and text

  • Body text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 against its background, and large text at least 3 to 1. Free contrast checkers will measure this for you.
  • Buttons, form borders and icons stand out from the background (at least 3 to 1).
  • Color is never the only signal. A sale price, an error or an out-of-stock size also uses words or a symbol.
  • Text can be enlarged to 200% without being cut off or overlapping.

3.Keyboard use

  • Everything that can be clicked can also be reached and used with the Tab key and Enter.
  • You can always see where you are: the selected link or button has a visible outline.
  • The order you move through the page makes sense and follows the layout.
  • There is a "skip to content" link at the top so keyboard users can jump past the menu.
  • Nothing traps you. Menus, pop-ups and the cart drawer can all be closed with the keyboard.

4.Page structure and links

  • Each page has one main heading, and headings follow a logical outline.
  • Each page has a clear, unique title in the browser tab.
  • Links say where they go. "Shop linen shirts" instead of "Click here."
  • Icon-only buttons, like the cart, search and menu icons, have a text label for screen readers.
  • The page's language is set, so screen readers pronounce it correctly.

5.Forms, cart and checkout

  • Every field has a visible label, not just placeholder text that disappears when you type.
  • Size, color and quantity pickers can be used with a keyboard and say which option is selected.
  • When an item is added to the cart, a screen reader hears about it.
  • Error messages say what went wrong and how to fix it, next to the field.
  • Shoppers are not timed out of the cart or checkout without a warning and a way to continue.

6.Pop-ups, sliders and motion

  • Pop-ups move focus into themselves when they open, can be closed with the Escape key, and return focus where it was.
  • Slideshows that move on their own can be paused.
  • Nothing flashes more than three times a second.
  • App widgets (reviews, chat, upsells, loyalty) pass the same checks as the rest of the page.

7.Policies and records

  • An accessibility statement is linked in your footer, with a monitored email address and phone number. Use our free template.
  • You keep dated records: when you checked the store, what you found, what you fixed and when.
  • You re-check after you change themes, install an app or launch a new page template.
  • You don't rely on an overlay widget in place of fixing the code (see why).

Automated tools can check part of this list for you; our free accessibility check runs the WCAG 2.2 Level A and AA checks that demand letters cite. The rest needs a person, ideally one who uses a keyboard and a screen reader. For what the law itself requires, read our ADA website compliance guide.
